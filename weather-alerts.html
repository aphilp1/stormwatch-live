The National Threat System (NTS) checks the national alert feed every 15 minutes and asks: are multiple hazard families simultaneously unusual right now, compared with the last 18 years? It groups related hazard types — severe storms, flooding (incl. coastal/lakeshore), wind (incl. marine advisories like Small Craft), fire weather, heat, winter, tropical, and coastal (fog/surf/rip current/tsunami) — into 8 families; a family counts only when its most-active member is itself at record/near-record levels, so one Coastal Flood Advisory or Small Craft Advisory breaking a record is enough on its own. A few rare hazard types (Air Quality Alert, Special Weather Statement, Beach Hazards Statement) have no 18-year baseline yet and stay out of every family until one is built. The State Activity Anomaly map (below) measures something different — a state's TOTAL alert volume of every type versus its own history — so the two can still legitimately disagree.

Method & sources

Method. Each hazard's current nationwide count is compared with the distribution of that hazard's counts at the same month and 6-hour window across 2008–present. The result is an empirical exceedance percentile (a "surprise" score); Extraordinary means roughly the top 0.1% — or a new 18-year record — for that calendar slot. A magnitude filter keeps rare-but-tiny anomalies (a lone off-season winter alert, or 3 Dense Fog Advisories nationally) from raising the level even if their percentile is extreme. The threat level counts how many of eight hazard families (severe, flood, wind, fire, heat, winter, tropical, coastal) are elevated at once — widened 2026-07-27 to include advisory-tier members (Small Craft, Coastal/Lakeshore Flood, Gale, High Surf, Rip Current, Tsunami) that were previously excluded from every family.

Data. Live alerts come from the NOAA/National Weather Service public alerts feed; the 18-year baseline (~162,000 hourly snapshots, 2008–present) comes from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet's curated NWS Watch/Warning/Advisory archive.

Sources & methods:
• NOAA/NWS Alerts (CAP) API — api.weather.gov
• Iowa Environmental Mesonet, Iowa State University — mesonet.agron.iastate.edu
• Climatological percentile / anomaly framework — Wilks, Statistical Methods in the Atmospheric Sciences, 3rd ed. (2011).

What is CAMS?
Convection-Allowing Models (CAMs) run at 3 km grid spacing or finer — fine enough to explicitly simulate individual thunderstorm updrafts and downdrafts. Coarser models (12–25 km) must parameterize convection using statistical rules; CAMs actually resolve it.

NOAA's National Severe Storms Lab (NSSL) runs several CAMs daily and posts side-by-side comparisons so forecasters can assess agreement and skill against real observations.

HRRR's 3 km grid resolves synoptic wind patterns but misses what happens at the terrain scale — the ridge amplification, canyon channeling, and gap flows that determine wind speed where fires actually burn.
WindNinja is a mass-consistent, terrain-following wind solver. Feed it an atmospheric boundary condition and a digital elevation model — it computes a physically consistent wind field at the resolution of the DEM (30 m here; can go finer).
